Dad kept the boat in a shed behind the Lutheran church, which nobody had questioned in eleven years because he also fixed the church's furnace. When I got there on Saturday he was on his back under the trailer with a socket wrench and a coffee can of bolts.

"Hand me the nine-sixteenths."

I found it on the second try. He didn't say anything about the first try.

"Your mother called," I said.

"I figured." He grunted at something. "She tell you about the house?"

"She said you're selling it."

"She's selling it. I'm signing where she points." He slid out, sat up, and wiped his hands on his jeans, which made them dirtier. "You want a Coke? There's Cokes in the cooler. Might be warm."

They were warm. We drank them anyway, sitting on the trailer fender, looking at the boat. It was a fourteen-foot Lund with a dent in the bow from the time Uncle Pete tried to dock it drunk at Lake Shetek. The dent had been there my whole life. Somebody had painted a little fish over it, badly, and then the paint had peeled, so now it was a dent with half a fish.

"You could take it," Dad said.

"Where would I put a boat? I live on the fourth floor."

"Lots of people keep boats in the city."

"Name one."

He thought about it. "Well, they must," he said, and laughed, and I laughed too, because he was right that they must, and because it was the worst argument I had ever heard him make. Then he went quiet and turned the can around in his hands.

"It's a good boat," he said.
